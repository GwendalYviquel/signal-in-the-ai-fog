# Forecasting on CPUs, diffusion speed, and a Mistral test

_2026-10-20_

Mistral releases Timestral, an open time-series forecasting model that runs on plain CPUs. Also: Inception's Mercury 3 diffusion model and its pricing, Mistral Large 4 open weights due October twenty-seventh, an MLflow security flaw to check, and the December second AI Act marking deadline.

## Script

Good morning, it's Tuesday, October 20. This is Signal in the AI Fog, your daily briefing on enterprise AI in production, written and read by AI. Today: Mistral's Timestral forecasts on plain CPUs, Inception's Mercury 3 launches with discounted pricing, and Mistral Large 4 weights due October 27.

First, the story of the day.

Mistral has released Timestral, its first family of time-series foundation models. It comes in four sizes: five million, twenty-eight million, one hundred twenty-three million and two hundred nine million parameters. Weights are on Hugging Face under a Modified MIT licence, and the inference code is under Apache 2.0. Mistral says the largest model ranks first among pretrained zero-shot models on three public benchmarks: GIFT-Eval, TIME and fev-bench. Those results are vendor-reported, and the technical report is still to come. No GPU is needed. On an eight-thread server CPU, a one-day-ahead forecast from three weeks of fifteen-minute history takes about one hundred forty milliseconds on the largest model, and ten on the smallest. It accepts multivariate inputs and known-future covariates, and returns twenty-one quantiles. On-premises, VPC and air-gapped deployment are supported, and fine-tuning goes through Mistral Forge.

What this really signals: forecasting is the quiet workhorse of retail AI, and here is a credible open model that runs on commodity CPUs, inside your own perimeter. For a platform team, that means no GPU queue, no data leaving, and a cheap serving profile next to your language-model stack. It is also a test of sovereignty beyond chatbots. The caveats are real. Benchmarks are self-reported, public leaderboards rarely capture your promotions and stock-outs, and a modified licence deserves a legal read. A sensible first step: backtest the small sizes against your current demand forecasts, judged on quantile calibration.

Next, Models and vendors.

Inception released Mercury 3 on October nineteenth. It is a diffusion language model with a one-million-token context window. List price is twenty cents per million input tokens and seventy cents per million output tokens, with fifty percent off at launch. It is available through Inception's API, OpenRouter, Baseten and Vercel. The speed and benchmark claims are Inception's own, and Artificial Analysis says the launch scores don't follow its methodology.

Reading between the lines: speed is the pitch, and it matters most for agents, where latency compounds across chained calls. But the quality claims are self-reported. For a gateway team, this is a candidate for latency-sensitive routes. Trial it against your own evaluation set while the launch discount lasts, and judge it on task success, not on tokens per second.

Next, Sovereignty and Europe.

Mistral Large 4 has been in preview on Mistral Studio since October sixth, with open weights due October twenty-seventh. It has one trillion parameters, forty-nine billion active, and was trained on four thousand Nvidia GPUs in Mistral's European data centres. CNBC reports it still trails the frontier in coding. Meanwhile, Ecosia has dropped Mistral for open-weight models including Chinese ones, citing quality. On October fifteenth, Arthur Mensch argued that open weights are only a partial answer to sovereignty.

The bigger picture: Europe's best open model will now be judged by customers who treat sovereignty as a feature, not as a purchase criterion by itself. If quality lags, buyers drift to cheaper open alternatives. For a platform team, the practical move is to run Large 4 through your evaluation harness the day weights land, and keep routing flexible.

Next, Platform.

MLflow 3.17.0, released October sixth, adds fine-grained permissions on runs, traces and versions, with wildcard grants and explicit deny. It also adds typed feedback for judges and scorers, and optional daily summaries for faster trace analytics. A 3.17.1 patch followed on October thirteenth. A path traversal flaw, CVE-2026-53995, affects the FileStore tracking backend in 3.17.1 and earlier, and lets unauthenticated callers abuse the traces endpoint.

Why it matters: if MLflow sits behind your gateway and evaluation stack, it is now critical infrastructure. Deployments on a database backend are likely less exposed than FileStore ones, but verify that rather than assume. Check your backend, restrict network access to the tracking server, and watch for a patched release before upgrading.

Next, Regulation.

The Digital Omnibus on AI has been in force since July twenty-seventh. It moved stand-alone high-risk obligations to December second, twenty twenty-seven. Article 50 transparency duties have applied since August second. Generative systems already on the market before then have until December second, twenty twenty-six, to add machine-readable marking. Deployer-side disclosure duties have no grace period. Signatories of the Code of Practice must have interoperable watermark detection by February second, twenty twenty-seven.

What to do with this: the December date is about six weeks away, and it covers providers, so check whether you count as one for any system you built or fine-tuned. Test that marking survives cropping, compression and reformatting in your real pipelines. Pick a method that can interoperate, to avoid doing the work twice.

The number of the day.

One thousand sixty-eight tokens per second: the speed Inception reports for Mercury 3 on Nvidia GPUs. It is a vendor-measured figure, so test it on your own workloads.

What to watch.

October twenty-seventh: Mistral Large 4 open weights are due, so expect early independent evaluations. Watch for a patched MLflow release addressing the FileStore flaw. Mistral's Priority Tier, with its uptime guarantee, has been in public preview since August, so general availability is worth tracking. December second is the Article 50 marking deadline for existing generative systems. February second, twenty twenty-seven, brings watermark interoperability.

And a question for your team.

If a cheap, fast model like Mercury 3 or an open model like Large 4 landed in your gateway tomorrow, how fast could you prove it is good enough?
